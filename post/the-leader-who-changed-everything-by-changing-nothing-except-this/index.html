The Leader Who Changed Everything By Changing Nothing Except This

By Adam McCutchen, May 18, 2026

Randy sat in a conference room at 4:47 on a Tuesday afternoon, staring at a project update from his team member Deja. The work was good. Not perfect, but genuinely good. He typed "looks fine, send it" and was three seconds from hitting send when something made him stop. He couldn't name it yet. He just knew that two words on a screen weren't going to be enough.

He deleted the message and typed something different: "Deja, this section on the client timeline is exactly the kind of thinking we need more of. Well done. Let's talk tomorrow about expanding this approach for Q3." He hit send and didn't think much more about it. But Deja did. She thought about it all week.

Here's the hard truth most leaders are avoiding right now: you already have more influence over your team's culture than any company initiative, any offsite retreat, or any values poster on the wall. The question isn't whether you have the power to build people up. The question is whether you're choosing to use it.

The Moment Is Already Here

Leadership isn't waiting for you at the top of some future mountain. It's happening right now, in your Tuesday afternoon inbox.

The "I" in IGNITE starts with identifying your leadership moments, and they are already everywhere. Every reply you write. Every hallway conversation. Every time someone shows you their work and waits for your response. These are not small moments. To the person on the receiving end, they can be defining ones.

Randy didn't have a plan that afternoon. He just made a choice. That's where culture change actually lives: not in strategy decks, but in individual choices made quietly, repeatedly, over time.

Most leaders, if they're honest, know they're under-investing in affirmation. Not because they're cruel, but because the pace of work makes it easy to skip. Busyness is the most socially acceptable reason to withhold encouragement, and it's also the most damaging one.

One Skill, Not a Complete Overhaul

The "G" in IGNITE is about growing one skill at a time, and this week, that skill is specific recognition. Not vague, not performative, not the "Great job, team!" blast email that everyone ignores. Specific, timely, personal recognition that tells someone exactly what they did well and why it matters.

There is a research-backed difference between "Good work" and "The way you structured that client presentation made a complicated concept easy to follow, and I think that's why they signed." One closes a loop. The other opens a door. The door leads to someone thinking, "I can do that again. I want to do that again."

Randy wasn't trying to fix culture that Tuesday. He was just trying to be more accurate about what he actually saw. That's the whole skill: learning to say out loud what you're already thinking silently when someone does something right.

Start there. Just there. One skill, practiced in real moments, changes everything downstream.

The Messy Middle Nobody Talks About

Here's where we have to be real, because the "N" in IGNITE is about navigating the hard stuff honestly.

Building people up is not always natural. For some leaders, it feels uncomfortable, even performative at first. If you grew up in environments where praise was scarce, where stoicism was strength, or where results were the only currency that counted, then learning to affirm others can feel like learning to walk in a new direction. Your instincts will push back. You might feel like you're being soft, or like you're lowering standards by celebrating ordinary work.

You're not. Recognizing someone's growth doesn't mean pretending every output is exceptional. It means you're paying close enough attention to see what's real. That's not softness. That's precision. And it requires more discipline than staying quiet ever does.

Randy had been managing Deja for eight months. He knew she was capable. But he'd never said it in a way that was specific enough to stick. The messy middle for him wasn't dramatic conflict or a hard conversation. It was the slow, quiet damage of a competent person who didn't know if her leader actually saw her. That kind of erosion is silent, and it's common, and most leaders don't catch it until someone leaves.

What You Can Do Today

The "Im" in IGNITE is about implementation, and this is where most leadership content lets you off the hook by staying abstract. Not here.

Today, before end of business, identify one person on your team who has done something right this week. Not something extraordinary. Something right. Then write them a message or tell them face-to-face, with this structure: what they did, why it mattered, and one forward-looking word about what you expect from them going ahead.

That's it. Three parts. Two minutes. Done.

If you can't think of anyone, that's your data point. It means either you're not paying attention closely enough, or your team has gone quiet in a way that deserves your immediate curiosity.

Randy made a habit of this. Not every day, because that would become noise. But intentionally, consistently, a few times a week. Within a month, something shifted. Deja started flagging ideas she would have kept to herself before. Another team member, Marcus, asked if he could take on a new project scope. The one-on-ones got more honest. Nobody had a meeting about culture. Culture just started to move.

Tracking the Shift

The "T" in IGNITE is about tracking your evolution, and culture change is one of the hardest things to measure because it shows up in texture, not metrics. But you can track your own behavior, and that's where the data lives.

Keep a simple log this month. Every time you give specific recognition, note it. Who, what, when. At the end of four weeks, look at the pattern. Who is showing up on your list regularly? Who isn't? That absence tells you something important about where your attention has been going.

Randy started doing this and quickly realized he had three people he'd essentially been leading on autopilot. Good performers who ran smoothly and, because of it, got the least of his intentional investment. High performers often get starved of acknowledgment because they don't seem to need it. They do. Everyone does.

Elevating Others Is the Point

The "E" in IGNITE lands here, and it reframes everything: leadership is not about how well you perform. It's about how fully you develop the people around you.

Randy's shift didn't make him look smaller. It made his team look bigger. And when a team looks bigger, so does the leader who built it. This is not a soft leadership principle. It is the most strategic thing you can do with your influence.

The leaders who build cultures worth being part of are not the ones who hoard recognition to maintain authority. They're the ones who give it away freely, specifically, and often because they understand that an affirmed team is a productive team, a loyal team, and a team that will walk through hard seasons with you.

Your people are watching you. Not just in the big meetings and the hard conversations. They're watching your Tuesday afternoon email replies. They're tracking, consciously or not, whether you see them.

See them. Say it. Watch what happens.

Ignite your potential, lead with purpose, and lead the kind of people who make others want to stay.

The Leader Who Knows Better (And Still Chooses Wrong)

By Adam McCutchen, June 4, 2026

Steven sat across from Marcus at the small conference table, the door closed, the conversation already uncomfortable before it even started.

Marcus was one of his best people on paper. Sharp, experienced, liked by the team. But for the third time this quarter, he had taken credit for a project that belonged to someone else. And the worst part? When Steven brought it up six weeks ago, Marcus nodded, said all the right things, and promised it would stop. Then it happened again. Then again.

Steven had come prepared with facts, timelines, and documentation. What he had not prepared for was the look on Marcus's face. It was not defiance. It was not denial. It was something far more complicated: Marcus knew exactly what he had done, and he looked like a man who hated himself for it.

That moment right there is one of the most disorienting places a leader can stand.

When Awareness Isn't Enough

Here is what leadership books rarely tell you: knowledge does not guarantee change.

We assume that if someone understands what they are doing is wrong, the fix is simple. Point it out, give them a chance to correct it, and they will. But human behavior is messier than that. Marcus knew he was wrong before Steven said a word. He knew it when he submitted the report. He probably knew it when he started planning it. And he did it anyway.

This is not a knowledge problem. It is a pattern problem, and patterns have roots.

Some people repeat wrong behavior because the short-term reward still outweighs the long-term consequence in their mind. Some do it because they have never been held to a real standard before. And some, like Marcus, do it because there is a gap between who they want to be and who they currently are, and they do not yet know how to close it. As a leader, your job is to navigate that gap with them without pretending it is not there.

Navigate the Messy Middle

The N in IGNITE is Navigate, and this is exactly the kind of moment it was built for.

Navigating is not fixing. It is not rescuing. It is not threatening consequences until someone complies. Navigating means you hold the tension of two truths at the same time: this behavior cannot continue, and this person is worth the hard work of a real conversation.

Steven made a choice in that room. He put down his documentation and asked Marcus a different kind of question: "What gets in the way for you when the moment comes?" Not "why did you do this again?" Not "do you understand how serious this is?" He asked Marcus to describe his own internal experience, because that is where the actual problem lived.

Marcus was quiet for a long time. Then he said something that shifted everything: "I panic when I think someone is going to take my seat."

The Real Work Starts Here

That admission did not excuse the behavior. Steven made that clear. The pattern had hurt a teammate, damaged trust on the floor, and could not keep happening regardless of the reason behind it.

But now Steven had something to work with.

Leading someone who knows they are doing wrong and still struggles to do right is not a discipline problem at its core. It is a development problem wrapped in a fear problem. Marcus did not need a longer lecture. He needed accountability structures that made it harder to act from panic, and he needed someone willing to stay in the conversation past the uncomfortable part.

Steven set three things in motion that day. First, Marcus would personally acknowledge the team member whose work he had taken credit for, privately and specifically. Second, they would meet weekly for thirty days, not to monitor Marcus, but to process the moments before they became patterns. Third, Steven was honest: if the behavior happened again, the conversation would change in nature. Not a threat. A boundary.

That is what navigating looks like. It is direct without being cruel. It is compassionate without being soft. It does not pretend the problem is small, and it does not treat the person as if they are beyond reach.

What You Can Do Today

Think about someone on your team right now who is struggling to do the right thing, even when they seem to know what it is.

Before your next conversation with them, ask yourself this question: am I treating this like a knowledge problem or a pattern problem? If they already know the right answer and still are not choosing it, more information is not the solution. A deeper conversation is.

Write down one question you could ask that invites them to describe their own internal experience. Not "why did you do that?" but "what happens inside you right before you make that call?" That one shift can change the entire temperature of the room.

Then hold the line. Be clear about what changes and by when. Show up for the follow-up. Do not walk away from the conversation and call it done.

The messy middle is not the failure. Refusing to enter it is.

Leading people through their own worst habits is hard, slow, unglamorous work. It will test your patience and occasionally make you question your own judgment. But when someone like Marcus sits across from you with that look on his face, the look that says he knows and still cannot seem to stop, that is not a sign to walk away. That is your call to lead.

Ignite your potential, lead with purpose, and don't stop leading when the leading gets hard.

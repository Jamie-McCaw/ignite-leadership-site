Going Against the Grain

By Adam McCutchen, May 26, 2026

Katie sat in the third all-hands meeting that month, where her director took credit for her team's work.

Not a vague, maybe-I-misheard-it kind of credit grab. A full, slide-by-slide walkthrough of the campaign Katie's team had spent six weeks building, presented as though her director had been in the trenches with them. He hadn't been. He'd shown up for the final review, suggested one font change, and apparently decided that was enough.

Katie looked around the room. Her team was watching her. Not their director. Her.

That moment, quiet and loaded, was a leadership moment nobody trained her for.

Toxic cultures don't always announce themselves. They don't come with warning labels or orientation packets that say, "By the way, your ideas will be stolen, your wins will be minimized, and your worth will be measured by how small you can make yourself." They build slowly, one dismissed suggestion, one rewritten email, one stolen presentation at a time.

And here's what nobody tells you: the hardest part of leading through a toxic environment isn't the toxicity itself. It's figuring out who you're going to be while you're inside it.

Katie had two options in that moment. She could shrink, swallow it, and protect her job by protecting her silence. Or she could choose, quietly and deliberately, to lead differently than the culture around her was modeled.

She chose the second one. And the way she did it mattered more than the choice itself.

After the meeting, Katie didn't march into her director's office, and she didn't fire off a frustrated email to HR. She did something smaller, and in the long run, far more powerful. She pulled her team together in the break room for fifteen minutes. No agenda. No slides. She looked at each person on her team and named, out loud, exactly what they had built and what it had cost them.

She said, "That campaign was yours. Every late night, every revision, every good idea in that deck came from this group, and I see it even when no one else does."

That was it. No dramatics. No promises she couldn't keep. Just a leader choosing to reflect back to her people the truth about their own value.

Her team didn't forget that moment. You won't forget the person who sees you when the system doesn't.

Leading through a toxic culture is not about fixing the culture overnight. It's not about becoming a whistleblower or a martyr or the one who sends the strongly-worded email that goes absolutely nowhere. It's about deciding, every single day, that the culture stops with you.

That means you give credit loudly and consistently. You protect your team's time and energy from the noise above you as much as you can. You tell the truth even when the environment rewards vagueness. You name good work, because in a culture that extracts from people, recognition is a radical act.

None of this requires a title change, a policy update, or permission from the person making things toxic. It requires one decision, made on repeat, to be a different kind of presence in the room.

You probably already know what that decision looks like for your team.

Here's your move for the next 24 hours.

Think of one person on your team who has done something worth seeing this week, and chances are they've gone unrecognized for it. Send them a direct message, leave a voicemail, or pull them aside today. Don't make it a big deal. Just make it specific. Name the exact thing they did, tell them why it mattered, and mean it.

That's it. Not a team email. Not a shoutout in a meeting they won't remember. A real, one-to-one, this-is-what-I-saw moment.

This is the Implement step, and it's available to you right now, today, inside whatever culture you're navigating. You don't wait for the culture to change before you start leading well. You lead well and let that be the change.

Toxic cultures lose their grip when enough people decide they'd rather ignite something than endure it.

That's how you lead with purpose.

Why Do You Lead?

By Adam McCutchen, August 14, 2026

Samuel was three minutes late to the Monday standup, coffee in hand, already thinking about the client call at ten. He slid into his seat just as Priya, the newest member of his team, started explaining why the dashboard numbers looked off. Her voice was tight. She kept glancing at her laptop instead of the room.

Samuel could have let it go. The meeting had a packed agenda and Priya's explanation, while shaky, was technically correct. Instead he said, "Walk us through it slower. I want everyone to follow your logic, not just the conclusion." Priya's shoulders dropped an inch. Nobody in that room would have called what Samuel did a leadership moment. But it was.

The Question Beneath the Question

Most of us don't struggle to explain what we do. We can talk about our responsibilities, our teams, our goals, our programs, our projects. Ask Samuel about his role and he'd tell you without hesitating: he manages a team of ten, oversees onboarding, reports up to the VP of Ops.

But here's a harder question. Why do you lead? Not why did you accept your current position, and not why do you need your paycheck. Why do you feel compelled to influence, develop, serve, challenge, or guide other people at all?

There's a real difference between assignment and purpose. Your assignment might be "I manage a team of ten." Your purpose sounds more like "I believe people have far more potential than they realize, and I lead to help them discover it." One explains your job. The other begins to explain you.

Samuel hadn't thought about his own answer in months. He was too busy managing the calendar. But that Monday morning, without planning to, he answered it anyway. He led because he cared whether Priya felt safe enough to be wrong out loud in front of her peers.

I: Identify the Moments Already Happening

This is where the IGNITE Method starts, and it starts with a skill most leaders never practice: identifying the moments already unfolding around them. Leadership doesn't announce itself. It rarely shows up as a big speech or a bold decision. It shows up in the three seconds before you decide whether to interrupt, defend, dismiss, or lean in.

Samuel's moment with Priya wasn't scheduled. It wasn't in the meeting agenda. It was a choice buried inside an ordinary Tuesday, disguised as nothing important. That's exactly why so many people miss these moments, waiting for leadership to look like leadership.

Think about your own week. The direct report who paused before answering your question. The teammate who made a joke to deflect an awkward silence. The moment someone on your team almost said something true and then didn't. Those are leadership moments too, whether or not you were the one speaking.

Look Backward Before Looking Forward

Here's something worth knowing: your why usually leaves clues throughout your story. You don't have to manufacture your leadership purpose from nothing. Most of the time, it's already there, waiting to be noticed the same way Samuel noticed Priya.

Think about the best leader you ever had. What did they give you? Confidence, opportunity, safety, trust, something else entirely. Now think about the worst leader you ever experienced, and ask what was missing. Those two answers usually reveal something about what you actually value, not just what you were taught to value.

Samuel thought about his old manager, the one who used to ask "what did you learn this week" instead of "what did you finish this week." That question had stuck with him for years without him realizing why. It was the same question, in a different form, he'd just asked Priya.

Why This Matters More Than You Think

Here's the danger of not identifying these moments. You start believing leadership only happens in the big, visible stuff: the reorg, the strategic pivot, the all-hands announcement. So you save your energy for those events and miss the hundred small ones that actually shape how people experience working with you.

Your team doesn't remember your quarterly vision statement nearly as well as they remember whether you noticed them struggling and said something. Samuel didn't fix Priya's dashboard. He didn't even fully understand the technical issue. What he did was notice a person having a hard moment and decide that moment deserved his attention.

This connects directly to why you lead, because your reasons will show up precisely in these unscheduled moments. If you're leading from assignment alone, you'll miss them, since they don't advance your visibility or your title. If you're leading from purpose, you'll start seeing them everywhere, and you won't be able to unsee them again.

Navigating the Discomfort of Looking Closer

This kind of noticing isn't always comfortable. Once you start identifying leadership moments, you start noticing the ones you've missed too. The times you talked over someone. The feedback you avoided giving. The person who needed five more minutes of your attention and didn't get it.

That's the messy middle of this work, and it's real. You won't get every moment right, and looking closer means seeing your misses more clearly, not less. The goal isn't perfection, it's attention, and every moment you catch now is one more than you caught last month.

This Week's Challenge

Take ten minutes this week and write three short stories. A moment you felt most alive while leading. A leader who brought out the best in you. A leader who showed you exactly what you never wanted to become.

Then look at all three and ask yourself what value shows up repeatedly. Finally, finish this sentence: I believe ________, which drives me to ________, so that ________.

Don't worry about making it perfect. Clarity rarely arrives before reflection, it arrives because of it. Samuel scribbled his version on the back of a notebook page during lunch, and it wasn't polished, but it was true, and that was enough to start.

Your Action Step for Today

Before your next meeting, pick one moment where someone on your team seems uncertain, rushed, or hesitant, and pause long enough to actually notice it. You don't have to fix it or say something profound. Just notice it, and see what happens when you do.

Then, sometime today, write down one line answering why you lead. Not your job title. The real reason underneath it.

The Point of All This

Leadership gets much easier to navigate once you know what you're actually trying to create. Why do you lead? The honest answer probably isn't in your job description. It's in the moments you choose to see, the Priyas of your week, and the story you'd tell about the leader who shaped you most.

Ignite something this week.

Ignite your potential, lead with purpose.
